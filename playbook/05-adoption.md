# 5 · Adoption: Lighthouse → Playbook → Scale

The transformation's hardest problem is not technical. It is that you are asking skilled professionals to change how they work, in public, while skeptics watch for the first stumble. Mandates fail here in a specific way: they produce *compliance* — tools installed, boxes checked, usage curves that collapse the week attention moves elsewhere. What you want is *demand*. Demand is engineered.

## Phase 1 — Lighthouse (6–8 weeks)

Pick two or three teams. Selection criteria, in order: a respected skeptic-curious tech lead (their conversion is worth fifty slide decks), real delivery pressure (toy teams prove nothing), and work spanning at least two risk tiers (so the model is exercised, not demoed).

Give them disproportionate support: the best tooling configuration you can assemble, a direct line to whoever runs the platform, and explicit permission to spend learning time. **Baseline their metrics before they start** (ch. 6) — the lighthouse's entire value is a before/after nobody can argue with.

The leader's job in this phase is subtraction: remove procurement friction, legal ambiguity, and tool-access delays within days, not quarters. Every week a lighthouse engineer waits on an approval is a week the skeptics' narrative writes itself.

## Phase 2 — Playbook (2–4 weeks, overlapping)

Extract what the lighthouse teams actually did into a short, written, versioned playbook: working tool configurations, the risk-tier table as *they* applied it, eval-suite starters, review norms for agent-authored changes, and — critically — **what didn't work**. A playbook without documented failures reads as marketing and is discarded as marketing.

The playbook is a living repo, owned like a product. Teams adopting it file issues and PRs against it. The moment it becomes a PDF, it is dead.

## Phase 3 — Scale (quarters, not weeks)

- **Champions, not enforcers.** One volunteer per team who gets early tool access, a direct channel to the platform owners, and visible credit. Champions pull; program offices push; pull wins.
- **Office hours and show-and-tells, relentlessly.** A weekly hour where anyone brings a real task and leaves with a working approach converts more engineers than any training course. Internal demos of real wins — with the messy parts left in — build the "that could be my Tuesday" effect that drives organic adoption.
- **Enablement by role, not by tool.** Training that teaches "here is the tool" produces dabblers. Training that teaches "here is how *your* job is done agentically — a backend engineer's workflow, a reviewer's workflow, an on-call workflow" produces practitioners. Certify against the workflow, lightly, if your organization responds to credentials.
- **Make the default path the paved path.** New repos arrive with eval scaffolding, agent configuration, and tier routing pre-wired. Adoption through defaults is the only adoption that compounds without attention.

## Leading it: practice what you mandate

One asymmetry decides more than any program design: **whether the leaders use the tools themselves.** An executive who builds with agents weekly — visibly, including the failures — licenses the whole organization to climb the same learning curve. An executive who sponsors from a distance teaches everyone that this is this year's initiative, survivable by waiting. I code with these tools daily; when I ran this at organizational scale, that fact did more for adoption than the entire enablement budget.

## The curve that tells you it's working

Mandated adoption spikes, plateaus, and decays. Demand-driven adoption climbs, dips when attention moves (that dip is honest), then *keeps climbing* — because the practice now spreads engineer-to-engineer, carried by self-interest. When teams you never touched show up asking for the playbook, Phase 3 is over and you have an operating model, not a program.
